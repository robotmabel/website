The graph

Commands in, state out.

Teleop and autonomy publish onto the command bus; the actuator drivers and the sensor nodes publish back onto the state bus — /joint_states, /odom, the camera images, and the LiDAR scan. Boot the twin and one mabel_mujoco_sim node stands in for all of them. Consumers never touch CAN or RS-485; they see topics.

Hover any node or bus for what it does — or tap on touch.

One launch file

Two ways to boot.

mabel_bringup/robot.launch.py takes a single use_sim argument — the real robot is a federation of driver nodes; the sim is one monolithic node. The graph above is identical either way.

ros2 launch mabel_bringup robot.launch.py

A federated robot

Brings up base.launch.py, body.launch.py, arms.launch.py and sensors.launch.py as independent nodes — each owns one firmware bus and can be restarted alone.

B

ros2 launch mabel_bringup robot.launch.py use_sim:=true

Teleop, WBC, and Nav2 can't tell the difference — same topics, same types. Validate in sim, drop the flag, deploy. See Simulation.

Controllers

The control stack, on tape.

The swerve base, the lift, the hands, and the whole-body arm solver. Each is a module in controller/mabel — the clips are that exact code driving the twin, base motion clamped by the same tip-over / fall envelope the keyboard teleop uses.

swerve.py · strafe → strafe → spin-in-place
Holonomic base · swerve.py

Three modules, any direction.

A 3-module delta swerve (wheel radius 38.1 mm) maps a body twist (vx, vy, ω) to per-module steer angle and wheel speed via rigid-body kinematics vi = V + ω × ri. Two tricks keep it from scrubbing the floor: shortest-path steering (never sweep past ±90° — flip and drive backward instead) and a cosine anti-slip choke that kills drive power until the column catches up.

Z-lift & torso

Eye height on demand.

A cascaded lift gives 0.635 m of vertical travel; a pitching torso adds reach low. The clip shows them one at a time — lift first, then torso. Raising the lift raises the CoM — h = 0.443 + 0.432·z — so the base gets easier to tip; a direction-aware tip-over model shrinks the safe speed and braking limits as ~1/h (atip = g·d / h), forward braking conservative, lateral reversals agile.

Dexterous hands

Seventeen joints per side.

Each hand is an open-source ORCA hand — five fingers, tendon-routed, with abduction, MCP, and PIP joints modelled per finger. Open to a flat palm, curl to a full fist; the same position commands drive the simulated and the real hand, so grasps tuned here transfer straight across.

wbc.py · 18-DOF bimanual DLS IK
Whole-body IK · wbc.py

Both hands, one solve.

The upper body is an 18-DOF chain — lift + torso + 7-DOF arm + wrist per side — solved as one weighted damped-least-squares problem that tracks both 6-DOF palm targets at once. A null-space posture task keeps the body near a natural configuration, and weighting makes the lift and torso "lazy" so the arms lead and the body only joins in when it has to — a slow, coordinated reach. Also in the twin

Beyond the three.

A

Gravity compensation

An upper-body dynamics model computes the holding torques so the arms go back-drivable — drag them by hand, they stay put when released. The base for drag-to-move teleop.

B

Compliance & admittance

Arms run compliant; torso and lift run "lazy" (gravity-held, heavily damped). The base behaves as a virtual planar mass for force-driven whole-body motion.

C

Lazy-base follower

When the hand targets drift to the edge of the arms' workspace, the swerve base creeps to re-centre them — MABEL's analogue of root-translation handling in GMR / HoMMI.

D

Teleop replay

Vision Pro retargeting and keyboard teleop drive the very same twin, so a session can be replayed and debugged offline before it ever touches hardware.

E

Sensor sim

A 360° planar LiDAR (RPLIDAR A3, 0.2–25 m) plus head RGB-D are modelled in-scene for mapping, SLAM, and navigation development.

F

Reproducible by script

Every clip on this page regenerates from scripts/tools/render_web_gifs.py driving the real controllers — no hand-keyframing, fixed scenes, deterministic.

Sim-to-real

One model, two worlds.

The gap is closed by construction: the twin and the robot share geometry, share interfaces, and the controllers don't know which one they're talking to.

01
Single source of geometry

One URDF generates the MuJoCo MJCF, the on-robot kinematics, and the web's 3D viewer. The swerve module positions, wheel radius, and lift limits the controllers use are read straight from the same config — sim and hardware can't silently diverge.

02
The same controller code

Swerve IK, whole-body IK, the lazy base, and the tip-over safety all live in controller/mabel. The simulator imports them unmodified; deployment swaps the MuJoCo plant for the real RS-485 / CAN bus, not the math.

03
Tip-safety from measured geometry

The support triangle and CoM projection in the ZMP limiter are measured from the model (wheel contacts + subtree CoM). The robot inherits the same envelope, so "safe in sim" means safe on the floor.

04
Built for data

The furnished home is where teleoperated demonstrations get collected and replayed. Small embodiment gap, real graspable objects, identical interfaces — the twin is the front door to the learning pipeline.

The contract

Intent up, video down.

A single running server feeds every client at once. Operator intent arrives on one WebSocket; the robot's cameras leave as ordinary HTTP MJPEG, kept off the command socket so a heavy video decode can never delay control.

Why a bridge

Skip ROS to drive the robot.

ROS 2 is the robot's internal nervous system — but you don't want a headset or a phone speaking DDS. The bridge is the seam: apps speak a tiny app-friendly protocol, and the bridge does the robot-side work.

01
Teleop from Apple devices

Vision Pro and iPhone send JSON over a WebSocket and render MJPEG in a WKWebView — no ROS client, no DDS discovery, no message-type compilation on the device.

02
Test the sim on a Mac

One command (./run.sh) brings up physics + cameras on the laptop. Add --mock to drive synthetic hands and exercise the entire stack with no headset attached.

03
Same contract reaches the real robot

The WebSocket + MJPEG contract is hardware-agnostic and the control math is shared. Point a robot-side process at the same two ports and the apps connect unchanged — sim and hardware run identical logic.

sim_teleop_bridge.py

What the bridge actually does.

One process closes the operator→robot half of the loop. It receives the newest frame, turns it into robot motion, and publishes the result for the cameras to render.

A
Receive & adapt

Take the newest TeleopFrame off the socket and adapt the headset's hand model into the controller's AVPFrame — each joint's world pose is anchorTransform · localTransform.

B
Retarget & solve

Run the shared whole-body controller — clutch-based hand retargeting → whole-body IK, plus a lazy swerve base and a look-at neck. The same controller the desktop viewer uses, so they can't drift.

C
Step in real time

Advance MuJoCo under a real-time accumulator that matches simulated time to wall-clock — clamped, so the robot never builds a latency backlog. mj_step is ≈ 3.9 ms for the 116-DOF model.

D
Publish qpos

Write the robot's qpos to a memory-mapped file. The camera box and the optional desktop viewer mmap it and mirror the robot — physics and video run on separate cores, in parallel.

The key latency decision: the bridge keeps a one-slot “newest frame” buffer and drops stale frames. Effective control latency is the uplink period (16.7 ms at 60 Hz, 50 ms at 20 Hz) plus one physics substep — never a queue backlog. On macOS the GL context must live on the main thread, so physics + IK + render run there while an asyncio thread hosts the WebSocket and MJPEG servers; they share only that one-slot buffer.

The wire protocol

Eight messages, mirrored in Swift.

Every message is a two-layer JSON envelope, { "type", "payload" }, with field names that match the Swift Codable types byte-for-byte — so a client and the bridge can never silently disagree. The Python side is stdlib-json only, so it drops straight into a ROS 2 node.

01
Discovery, no IP typing

The server advertises _mabel-teleop._tcp over Bonjour; clients browse and resolve it. Pick the server from a list — or type the Mac's LAN IP.

02
Resilient by design

An 8 s handshake watchdog, a 5 s non-fatal ping, and auto-reconnect with linear backoff capped at 4 s. One dropped frame out of a 20 Hz stream never tears the session down.

03
Forward-compatible

A new message is one enum entry on each side; unknown types are rejected at decode, so compatibility is explicit rather than silent.

Reaching the robot

Three ways in, one Connect button.

The same two-port contract is reachable over three paths, and every client — Vision Pro, iPhone, and the browser console — just tries them in order. No mode switch, no second app.

01
Wi-Fi · same LAN

The Mac's LAN IP on :9090 / :8080 — lowest latency, advertised over Bonjour (_mabel-teleop._tcp) so clients resolve it without typing an IP.

02
VPN · same tailnet

The Mac's Tailscale IP / ts.net name reaches it from any network with no public exposure. From an https page a bare host upgrades to the TLS proxy (tailscale serve --https=8443).

03
Relay · from anywhere

A token-gated secure internet relay: the robot dials out to a public VPS, so anyone can drive from anywhere with just a hostname and a token — the control loop never faces the internet.

The secure relay

Dial out, gate hard.

The robot opens an outbound rathole tunnel to the VPS (piercing enterprise NAT & client isolation — outbound is always allowed). Caddy is the only public port: auto-TLS, and every request must carry the secret ?key=<APP_TOKEN> or it gets a 403 before reaching the tunnel. Video rides the same token-gated HTTPS edge, so there's no public UDP port to flood.

The video channel

Cameras as plain HTTP.

Each camera is a standalone multipart/x-mixed-replace MJPEG stream the headset pulls into a WKWebView <img> — the exact path it would use for a hardware camera. Rendered in a separate process because the off-screen MuJoCo render is a fixed per-frame GPU-readback stall (≈73 ms with shadows, ≈41 ms without); running it on the physics thread would starve real-time control. The feeds render shadows off by default — the shadow pass alone costs ~32 ms/frame, so dropping it nearly doubles the budget and keeps the head and wrist streams responsive.

The cascade

Three nested loops, one motion.

A command doesn't go straight to the motors. It descends through three loops nested one inside the next — the outer one slow and soft, the inner one fast and precise. Each takes a clear input, does one job, and hands a clear output to the loop inside it. Sensed force climbs back out the way it came.

Mode 1 · Gravity compensation

The arm floats.

A full rigid-body model computes the gravity torque G(q) for every link and feeds it forward, so each joint exactly holds its own weight. The arms become weightless and back-drivable — guide them with a fingertip and they stay where you leave them. This is the inner loop's feedforward term, and the backbone of kinesthetic teaching and safe shared workspaces.

Under the hood

Redundancy, resolved.

Fifty-one joints, one objective. Here's how the extra freedom gets used.

01
Null-space posture

Base, lift, torso, and arm joints solve jointly. The spare freedom maximizes manipulability and stays clear of joint limits — MABEL rarely paints itself into a corner.

02
Mobile base in the loop

The holonomic base is part of the reach. Far target? cuRobo drives the body. Fine work? Hold still and let the wrist talk.

03
Bimanual coupling

The ACM masks hand-to-hand collision pairs so the two hands can cooperate — carry a tray, open a lid — without the safety filter fighting itself.

04
Torque at the actuator

Quasi-direct-drive motors close a high-rate current loop, so commanded torques become real ones — the foundation gravity comp and admittance stand on. See firmware for the bus and timing.

05
Perception in the safety loop

The ZED Mini point cloud becomes an nvblox ESDF the middle loop checks every candidate against — the obstacle map and the motion plan live in the same GPU memory. See software.

06
Shared with everything

The same cascade serves teleop and learned policies. They supply intent; the cascade makes it feasible, safe, and smooth.

Mode 1 · Gravity compensation

The arm floats.

A full rigid-body model computes the gravity torque g(q) for every link and feeds it forward, so each joint exactly holds its own weight. The arms become weightless and back-drivable — guide them with a fingertip and they stay where you leave them, with no spring pulling back. The torso and lift run lazy (gravity-held, heavily damped); the base, neck, and hands stay locked. This is the backbone of kinesthetic teaching and safe shared workspaces.

Mode 2 · Whole-body compliance

Push the arm, move the robot.

Gravity comp keeps the arms back-drivable; the external wrench is then estimated from joint torque (a generalized-momentum observer maps τext through (JT)# to a Cartesian force — no force sensor) and fed to a base admittance. The base behaves as a virtual planar mass-damper, m v̇ = fext − b v, with a Coulomb deadband and a kinetic stop: push an arm past its comfortable workspace and the whole robot glides along with you, then coasts to a stop when you let go — it neither springs back nor rolls forever. The exact same code runs on hardware off the motor torque feedback.

Under the hood

Redundancy, resolved.

Fifty-one joints, one objective. Here's how the extra freedom gets used.

01
Null-space posture

Base, lift, torso, and arm joints solve jointly. The spare freedom maximizes manipulability and stays clear of joint limits — MABEL rarely paints itself into a corner.

02
Mobile base in the loop

The holonomic base is part of the reach. Far target? Drive the body. Fine work? Hold still and let the wrist talk.

03
Bimanual coupling

A relative-pose constraint locks the two hands together while the body moves freely — carry a tray, open a lid, never drop the grasp.

04
Torque at the actuator

Quasi-direct-drive motors close a high-rate current loop, so commanded torques become real ones — the foundation gravity comp and impedance stand on. See ROS for the bus and timing.

05
Collision-aware planning

For arm and torso motions that need foresight, MoveIt2 plans collision-checked paths against the shared URDF; a small arbiter decides — per actuator group, per moment — whether the planner or the reactive controller holds authority.

06
One observation graph

Head RGB-D, wrist cameras, joint encoders, LiDAR, and IMU are time-synchronized into a single ROS 2 graph, so the controller, the planner, and any policy all read the same source of truth. See ROS.

07
Shared with everything

The same controller serves teleop and learned policies. They supply intent; the whole-body layer makes it feasible, safe, and smooth.
