Stage 1

Where am I?

A robot that does not know where it is cannot do anything else. Three answers, all measured against MuJoCo ground truth in the same room: wheels alone drift the moment one slips, vision corrects it, and the filter keeps whichever is currently telling the truth.

01 · WHEELS

Swerve forward kinematics

Three steered modules over-determine the body twist, so swerve_odometry least-squares them into one (vx, vy, ω) from /joint_states alone. Honest but slip-blind.

02 · LiDAR

Keyframe ICP

laser_odometry matches each scan to a keyframe rather than to the last scan, so error does not compound frame to frame. 720 points at 13 Hz costs 2–4 ms.

03 · VISION

cuVSLAM VIO

Stereo plus IMU on the GPU. Absolute pose, no drift with distance — the estimate the filter trusts.

04 · FUSION

The EKF, and its caveat

Takes velocity from the wheels and pose from vision, at 30 Hz in 2-D. It is tuned and benchmarked, but its launch file pins simulated time and no bring-up script starts it — built, not yet deployed.

The base itself is identified, not assumed. A system-ID sweep puts the real motion at 0.989 × commanded — 1.1% slip, identical on every axis, decoupled, linear down to 0.05 m/s, with a first-order velocity ramp of τ = 0.13 s. Modelling that ramp cut held-out error from 4.0% to 2.2% of distance travelled (4.7 cm over 2.16 m). It also reframes the wheel odometry's 3× over-read: the robot was stuck, not mis-modelled.

Stage 2

Two ways to build a map.

Drive it yourself and watch the map appear. MABEL builds its own floor plan from a 360-point LiDAR — no beacons, no prior map, no markers on the walls. Two stacks do it: a GPU visual one and a CPU LiDAR one, both publishing the same map → odom. The tool below is the CPU one, ported to run in this page.

And it can do this without you. MABEL runs a Yamauchi-style frontier explorer: it watches the live occupancy grid, finds the cells where known-free space touches unknown, clusters them, scores each cluster by travel cost less an information-gain bonus, and sends the best one to Nav2 as a NavigateToPose goal. Unreachable goals are blacklisted so it never loops on one, and when several cycles in a row turn up nothing it latches exploration_complete and returns home. It is deliberately SLAM-agnostic — its only inputs are /map and the map → base_link transform, so it works behind either stack below. Read the node ↗

cuVSLAM + nvblox · the GPU path

Two rectified stereo streams and an IMU into cuVSLAM; nvblox turns depth into a TSDF mesh and slices a 2-D occupancy layer out of it. Everything loads as composable nodes into one container so frames move by zero-copy.

slam_toolbox · the CPU path

One asynchronous lifecycle node on /scan, Ceres behind it, publishing map → odom at 50 Hz. Loop closure is on: it searches 3 m and needs a chain of ten scans before it will believe a match.

Real grids, built by casting MABEL’s own lidar (12 m, 720 bins) against the scene geometry and integrating log-odds at 5 cm, slam_toolbox’s own resolution — dark is occupied, cream is swept and empty, tan was never seen, and the red track is where the robot stood. This is mapping with known poses, not SLAM: the poses come from the simulator rather than from scan matching, so it is the grid a perfect front end would build. Regenerate with simulation/mabel_mujoco/scripts/tools/render_slam_maps.py.

We recommend the LiDAR path, and the robot already picks it. Bring-up listens for a /scan for 15 seconds. If one appears, slam_toolbox takes over the 2-D map and map → odom — cuVSLAM is switched off entirely and nvblox is demoted to 3-D mesh only, for the apps to draw. The reason is compute, not accuracy: LiDAR SLAM is CPU-only, while the visual stack wants the same GPU your policy does. nvblox cost scales with the cube of voxel size, so 5 cm is roughly 4× cheaper than 3 cm, and running the cameras at 15 fps instead of 30 roughly halves the load with no practical accuracy cost.

A saved room is four files: a 2-D .pgm+.yaml for Nav2, a .ply mesh for the apps, and a .nvblx for relocalisation. Frontier exploration can drive the whole survey and save at the end.

Stage 3

Every session is a demonstration.

The paper records stereo head and both wrist RGB streams at nominal 30 Hz, with available depth, plus timestamped joint feedback, commanded references, base twist and lift-jog commands at nominal 50 Hz. Raw timestamps and validity flags are retained. The demonstrated joint references q* supervise learning; base-twist and lift-jog fields are stored separately from the 59 joint coordinates.

Current hand-grasping dataset · v2. 80 takes, 117 curated episodes and 68,364 frames: 38.0 minutes at 30 fps. The 1.5 GB export stores four camera videos and joint data. Training uses the left head view and both wrists, with 240 × 320 cached images (height × width).

Stage 4

Cut it like film.

Review camera streams and robot state together, trim unwanted intervals and discard faulty episodes while preserving the raw recording. The browser example below lets you explore timing, frozen motion and missing frames in three sample takes.

Stage 5

ACT training and architecture tools.

ACT combines three camera views with one second of joint history to predict four seconds of actions. The v2 dataset is split 80 / 10 / 10 by take with seed 42. Every episode from a take stays in one split.

Stage 6

Run it, then judge it.

ACT action predictions use the same whole-body controller interface as teleoperation. It predicts four seconds of motion, executes the first second, then re-plans from new observations, without temporal ensembling.

Shared control path

Predicted joint-reference chunks include the finger and wrist targets. The controller routes them through the same control interface used by the operator, to hardware or the simulation bridge. The 59-coordinate representation counts the coupled lift as one height.

Validation and final test

Training checks use 384 validation clips every 500 steps. The test split stays separate until final evaluation. Overlapping clips are training samples, not additional minutes of demonstrations.

Grasp-and-place scope. The v2 run focuses on the hands and upper body. Its 59-coordinate state and 63-channel action schema retain the full representation; inactive lift, swerve and base-command channels are masked for training. Dataset size and open-loop validation do not establish a new autonomous success rate.
