Stage 1

Where am I?

Three ways to answer, measured against MuJoCo ground truth in the same room. Wheels alone drift badly the moment a wheel slips; vision fixes it; the filter keeps both.

EstimatorPosition errorHeadingPath length vs truth
Wheel odometry only0.67 m12.8°3.0× — slip reads as distance
Visual (cuVSLAM)0.21 m3.4°1.10×
Fused EKF0.21 m3.4°1.08×
01 · WHEELS

Swerve forward kinematics

Three steered modules over-determine the body twist, so swerve_odometry least-squares them into one (vx, vy, ω) from /joint_states alone. Honest but slip-blind.

02 · LiDAR

Keyframe ICP

laser_odometry matches each scan to a keyframe rather than to the last scan, so error does not compound frame to frame. 720 points at 13 Hz costs 2–4 ms.

03 · VISION

cuVSLAM VIO

Stereo plus IMU on the GPU. Absolute pose, no drift with distance — the estimate the filter trusts.

04 · FUSION

The EKF, and its caveat

Takes velocity from the wheels and pose from vision, at 30 Hz in 2-D. It is tuned and benchmarked, but its launch file pins simulated time and no bring-up script starts it — built, not yet deployed.

The base itself is identified, not assumed. A system-ID sweep puts the real motion at 0.989 × commanded — 1.1% slip, identical on every axis, decoupled, linear down to 0.05 m/s, with a first-order velocity ramp of τ = 0.13 s. Modelling that ramp cut held-out error from 4.0% to 2.2% of distance travelled (4.7 cm over 2.16 m). It also reframes the wheel odometry's 3× over-read: the robot was stuck, not mis-modelled.

Stage 2

Two ways to build a map.

A GPU visual stack and a CPU LiDAR stack, both publishing the same map → odom. Never run both — and on the real robot you do not have to choose, because the bring-up decides for you.

cuVSLAM + nvblox · the GPU path

Two rectified stereo streams and an IMU into cuVSLAM; nvblox turns depth into a TSDF mesh and slices a 2-D occupancy layer out of it. Everything loads as composable nodes into one container so frames move by zero-copy.

Voxel size0.05 m navigating · 0.01 m manipulating
Integration range4 m nav · 1.5 m manip
ESDF slice0.02–0.40 m band, cut at 0.13 m
Colour integration5 Hz nav · 15 Hz manip
slam_toolbox · the CPU path

One asynchronous lifecycle node on /scan, Ceres behind it, publishing map → odom at 50 Hz. Loop closure is on: it searches 3 m and needs a chain of ten scans before it will believe a match.

Resolution0.05 m
Laser range used0.45 – 12 m
New node every0.2 m or 0.2 rad
Map updateevery 0.5 s

Real grids, built by casting MABEL’s own lidar (12 m, 720 bins) against the scene geometry and integrating log-odds at 5 cm, slam_toolbox’s own resolution — dark is occupied, cream is swept and empty, tan was never seen, and the red track is where the robot stood. This is mapping with known poses, not SLAM: the poses come from the simulator rather than from scan matching, so it is the grid a perfect front end would build. Regenerate with simulation/mabel_mujoco/scripts/tools/render_slam_maps.py.

We recommend the LiDAR path, and the robot already picks it. Bring-up listens for a /scan for 15 seconds. If one appears, slam_toolbox takes over the 2-D map and map → odom — cuVSLAM is switched off entirely and nvblox is demoted to 3-D mesh only, for the apps to draw. The reason is compute, not accuracy: LiDAR SLAM is CPU-only, while the visual stack wants the same GPU your policy does. nvblox cost scales with the cube of voxel size, so 5 cm is roughly 4× cheaper than 3 cm, and running the cameras at 15 fps instead of 30 roughly halves the load with no practical accuracy cost.

A saved room is four files: a 2-D .pgm+.yaml for Nav2, a .ply mesh for the apps, and a .nvblx for relocalisation. Frontier exploration can drive the whole survey and save at the end.

Stage 3

Every session is a demonstration.

Teleoperate and you are recording. One shared 15 Hz clock indexes seven cameras, 59 joints, LiDAR and the operator's own targets into one episode file.

WHAT
One file per episode

HDF5, frame-indexed: JPEG colour, 16-bit depth in millimetres, a 360-point scan, joint position / velocity / torque, and the operator's palm targets and grasp.

LABEL
Absolute joint targets

action/ctrl is the command sent to all 59 actuators — absolute, not a delta. That is what a policy learns to produce.

SIZE
≈38 MB per minute

Against roughly 1.5 GB per minute per camera for a raw bag. Images are about 96% of it, which is why the format matters.

RAW
Bags are still there

An opt-in rosbag recorder captures 52 topics byte-for-byte for debugging. The curation studio does not read them.

Where this is honest about itself. The simulation collector is complete. On hardware, the working recorder is a standalone ROS node driven by the app's own "start collecting" button — it was verified end to end in July 2026, but with synthetic actions on a static robot, and the curation engine correctly scored that data as frozen. The tidier collect_real.py harness is still a stub with TODO hooks. Most collected data today is simulated.

Stage 4

Cut it like film.

A non-linear editor for demonstrations. Blade, ripple-delete, merge and reorder on a timeline — and the source files are never touched.

EDL

Nothing is destroyed

A project is an ordered list of (episode, start, end) segments. Saving materialises a new episode file, slicing every stream including the JPEG blobs and rebasing time to stay monotonic. Roughly 1.4 s for a typical cut.

QC

It finds what you would miss

Because everything shares one clock there is nothing to re-sync — so the engine hunts the failures that matter instead: control lag between action and observation (cross-correlated, correctable and gated at export), timing gaps against the episode's own cadence, frozen joints, zero torque, NaNs and black cameras.

OUT

LeRobot v2.1

Exports observation.state from measured joint positions, action from the commanded targets, and one MP4 per camera — the layout the trainer reads. A headless curate_export does the same from a shell and writes a per-episode quality report beside it.

A dataset-quality curation studio for MABEL imitation-learning data. SCOPE
Choose the body

Whole body, upper body, one arm, both hands, the base — the selection builds a real sliced dataset and a scatter map back onto all 59 actuators.

TRUTH
The filesystem is the database

Every run is a directory with its manifest, config, log, metrics and checkpoints. A run survives a database wipe; the registry rebuilds by scanning.

PROOF
Measured, on hardware

ACT (52 M parameters) and the diffusion policy (296 M) both train on the Jetson. On real curated data ACT went from L1 0.464 to 0.135 train, 0.572 to 0.136 validation.

YOURS

The community queue takes an architecture, a dataset and hyperparameters, clamps them to published ranges, and waits for approval before anything touches the GPU.

Stage 6

Run it, then judge it.

A checkpoint goes back into the same loop it learned from: proprioception and camera renders in, 59 actuator targets out, at 15 Hz over 500 Hz physics.

Closed loop, in the twin

Deployment loads the checkpoint through the standard policy path, reads joint positions straight out of MuJoCo, renders each selected camera, and scatters the policy's action onto the full 59-DOF command — joints outside the scope simply hold. Every frame streams back to the viewer.

Offline, against held-out episodes

The evaluation harness replays unseen episodes frame by frame and reports L1 and MSE per joint and overall, persisted beside the run. A self-test measured open-loop L1 of 0.183 and a closed-loop deployment that moved the robot.

What does not exist yet, stated plainly. Policies do not run on the physical robot. The wire protocol can select one and echo it in robot state, but the runner that would execute it is a documented seam, not code — every long-horizon skill in the control centre is declared "coming soon, no deployable checkpoint yet". And there is no task-success-rate harness for imitation policies: the metric today is open-loop action error, not "did it make the coffee". Navigation is the exception, with measured pose error and map accuracy.
